Fed Retires Dot Plot After 14 Years of Wrong Projections, Vows Future Will Be Wrong About Something Else

The scatter chart had one last request before it was phased out: that the committee note it was never the members who were poor at plotting, only the future.

PSPriya Sharma
Washington Bureau, Fed & Rates, plus household costs (insurance, mortgages) 2026-11-13 · 2 min read

Shelley Ng works in the publications office of the Federal Reserve Board. Her job is to format the quarterly Summary of Economic Projections, a document that contains seventeen pages of charts, footnotes, and projections that are wrong within six weeks of printing. She has done this for fourteen years. She has never once seen a projection survive a full quarter without being revised, apologized for, or silently replaced.

The dot plot was her least favorite page. Every quarter she would lay out nineteen tiny dots in a scatter chart that showed where each committee member thought rates would be in three years. Every quarter the dots would be wrong. Every quarter Shelley would format them anyway, because her job is not to be right but to make the wrongness look professional.

The Fed announced this week that the dot plot is being retired and replaced with a single comma. The comma will appear at the end of every FOMC statement. The Fed says the comma will be "inflected for tone" depending on the data. Shelley has been asked to format the comma. She asked her supervisor what "inflected" means. Her supervisor said it means they can make the comma slightly italic if they want to signal concern. Shelley asked if there was a comma bold for panic. Her supervisor did not laugh.

Shelley's actual opinion is that the comma is a better system because it says less and therefore cannot be as wrong. The dot plot projected rates at 2.5 percent for 2025. The actual rate was 5.5 percent. The comma, by comparison, never promised anything. It just sits there, on the page, admitting the sentence is ongoing, which is the most honest thing the Fed has printed in a decade.

“The dot plot was wrong for fourteen years. The comma has never been wrong. I like knowing I'm getting something honest. A comma just sits there and admits the sentence is not over. That's the most reliable thing the Fed has ever printed.”

— a Fed watcher who prefers the comma

Shelley will file the dot plot materials in the archive, which is where the Fed keeps everything it used to be sure about. The archivist is named Phil. He has been there thirty years. He told Shelley the comma was a good idea. He has seen longer systems fail.
