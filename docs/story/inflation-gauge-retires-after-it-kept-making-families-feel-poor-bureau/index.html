Inflation Gauge Retires After It Caught Diane Kowalski At The Checkout One Too Many Times

Diane was at the Kroger self-checkout in Toledo at 8:47 AM when her phone buzzed with the monthly CPI print, which confirmed that the orange juice she had already scanned was, economically speaking, a mistake. The Bureau, apologizing to Diane broadly, has decided the gauge needs a break.

PS Priya Sharma
Washington Bureau 2026-11-03 · 2 min read

Diane Kowalski was standing at the self-checkout at the Kroger on Secor Road at 8:47 in the morning with a cart that contained, among other things, a half-gallon of orange juice she had already scanned and a rotisserie chicken she had not yet committed to, when her phone buzzed with the monthly inflation print, which told her, in real time, that the orange juice had become 6.2 percent more expensive since she bought it last week. Diane looked at the phone. She looked at the juice. The juice was already scanned.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics confirmed that the headline CPI number, while technically correct, had caught Diane in a genuine moment of financial vulnerability, which the Bureau now understands is the entire month, and that the timing had become a pattern. The orange juice, the Bureau conceded, was not at fault. The data, arriving when it did, had made the juice feel like a decision Diane would have to defend, which is not what a Thursday morning at the grocery store is for.

“The data is accurate,” a Bureau spokesperson said from a desk that costs more than Diane’s rent. “What we failed to account for is that Diane did not need the data at that moment. She needed the juice. And the data, arriving when it did, made the juice feel like a luxury, which juice is not.”

Diane put the orange juice back on the return shelf, which is the shelf for items that were scanned and then reconsidered in the light of new economic information. She stood at the self-checkout for another moment, the rotisserie chicken still undecided, her phone still glowing with the month’s economic truth. The Bureau says it will replace the gauge with a new metric called the Affordability Comfort Index, which measures how much a family thinks it can afford, which the Bureau believes is higher than what it actually can, and therefore better for everyone involved.

Diane left the store with the chicken and without the juice. The Bureau, reviewing the tape, called that an acceptable compromise, and confirmed it would not send her the next CPI print directly to her phone, which Diane is grateful for, and which the Bureau confirmed is the closest it has ever come to an apology.
