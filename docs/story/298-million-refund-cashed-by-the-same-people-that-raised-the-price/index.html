$298 Million Refund Cashed by the Same People That Raised the Price

A company that blamed tariffs for jacking up prices gets the money back and routes it straight to the C-suite, because the machine doesn't correct itself.

MRMarcus Reid
Markets Anchor · NYSE Floor 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

On paper, a tariff refund is straightforward: the government collects a tax the courts later rule illegal, and the money is returned to the party that paid it. PrecisionEdge Tool Works, a Connecticut-based manufacturer of hammers, saws and power drills, is expecting at least $298 million in such refunds after the Supreme Court struck down the underlying tariffs. The company collected higher prices from its customers during the period the tariffs were in effect, citing the import costs as unavoidable. The customers paid those higher prices. The refund is now arriving.

PrecisionEdge disclosed in a securities filing that the tariff windfall would be “partially offset by directly attributable costs for variable incentive compensation,” a formulation that directs a portion of the refund to executive bonuses rather than to the customers who absorbed the original price increases. The company is not alone: at least nine publicly traded companies have disclosed similar arrangements, according to filings reviewed by analysts. The parent company of the ValueSavers discount department-store chain received $331 million in refunds and indicated the proceeds would flow into its bonus pool. Empire Golf, which sells premium equipment with drivers starting above $600, adopted a comparable structure.

“The CEO got a bonus from the $298 million tariff refund and good for him. He kept the company profitable through a trade war. If I ran a company that got that money back I'd take my share too.”

— a shopper in the ValueSavers parking lot, 43

The mechanics of the arrangement are internally consistent. Companies told consumers the tariffs were a cost of doing business that had to be passed along. The cost was passed along. The refunds are not being returned to consumers because the refund mechanism directs money to the importer of record, which is the company, not the end customer. Trade policy, as executed, collects money from purchasers and distributes it to corporate entities.

The ValueSavers parent company said it would “evaluate our pricing on a go-forward basis as market conditions evolve,” the standard formulation for prices not going down.

At press time, the government had refunded approximately $800 million to publicly traded companies, none of which had announced plans to forward the money to the customers who paid it. A spokesperson for the Treasury Department said the refunds had been “processed efficiently and in accordance with applicable law.”
