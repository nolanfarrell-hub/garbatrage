Average 30-Year Rate Reaches 7.4%, Affordable Home Now An Investment Thesis The Buyer Will Never Live To See Proven

With the average mortgage rate at a three-year high, the industry has confirmed the starter home is no longer bought, only inherited.

OZOmar Zayed
Corporate Governance Desk 2026-11-14 · 2 min read

AUSTIN, TX—The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate rose to 7.4 percent this month, its highest in three years, prompting the real-estate industry to formally reclassify the affordable starter home from a purchase into a generational heirloom, to be passed down unopened and preferably still occupied by someone who locked in a lower rate during the Obama administration.

"We stopped asking buyers whether they can afford the house and started asking them which child deserves to inherit the regret," said Marcus Webb, a loan officer whose own mother, he confirmed, holds the deed to a three-bedroom ranch in a trust that will release it only upon proof that nobody has lived in it for forty years. "The starter home is not started anymore. It is finished. It is finished the way a cathedral is finished, which is to say you may look at it but you may not live in it."

Ricky Ortez, 28, a warehouse worker in Austin, said he toured an open house last weekend and the agent handed him a laminated card explaining that the home was not for sale to him but was merely being exhibited, the way a museum exhibits a dinosaur to a child who will never own the dinosaur. "The card said 'this home is a legacy property,'" Ortez said. "I asked if I could leave a legacy of my own. The agent said I could leave my contact information."

“I told my daughter she was not going to buy this house because it was a legacy property, which meant it was meant for someone who already had it, and I was proud she would understand that someday when she inherited something from someone who was not her.”

— a homeowner who explained legacy to his daughter

At press time, the industry had announced its strongest product yet: a new 45-year mortgage designed, in the lender's phrase, for a family that will not "even be born into a position to start dying inside it" until two more generations have had a chance to look at the house first.
