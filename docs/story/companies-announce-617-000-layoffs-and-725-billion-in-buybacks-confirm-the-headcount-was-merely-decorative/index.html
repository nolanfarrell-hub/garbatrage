The 617,000 Laid Off This Year Were Named Raymond, A Call Most Of Them Answered With Earning Ceased

Net job cuts slowed 44% from last year's total, a statistic that confused only the recently separated, who could not see it from where they landed.

JS Jake Sullivan
Corporate Reporter 2026-11-15 · 2 min read

Raymond Hightower got the news at 10:30 on a Wednesday. He was one of 617,189 people laid off by US employers in the first ten months of the year. He found out via a notification on his phone from the HR portal, which he had installed when he was hired and had never used until it told him he was no longer employed. He read the message, put his phone down, and picked it back up to check if the number was lower than last year's. It was. That made him feel worse somehow.

The same morning, Raymond's former employer announced a $2.3 billion share buyback program, part of the $725 billion in total repurchases announced by US companies year to date. Raymond read about the buyback on his phone in the parking lot. He had owned zero shares of the company, because he had been a warehouse supervisor, not an investor. The stock, he learned, would be bought back at a price the company considered attractive. Raymond, whose position had been eliminated, was not considered attractive at any price.

Executives explained that the two figures were not in tension but in arrangement. The company was not eliminating positions, it was relocating the value that the positions had been, at great expense, failing to hold. Raymond asked if the value could be relocated to his bank account. He was told that was not how relocation worked.

“I asked if former employees counted as shareholders, and they said only if I owned stock, and I said I had worked there for six years, and they said that was not stock, and I realized they were right because showing up and owning were two completely different things.”

— a former warehouse supervisor who now understands the distinction

At press time, Raymond had filed for unemployment and was looking at the company's stock price, which had gone up two percent on the buyback news, a gain he could not participate in because his severance was not enough to buy a share.
