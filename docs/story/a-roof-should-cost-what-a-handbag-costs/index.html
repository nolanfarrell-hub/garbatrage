Insuring a Roof Now Costs More Than the Handbag It Protects

The state pool raised rates 29.1 percent. The average policy is $3,057.

PS Priya Sharma
Estate & Property Correspondent 2026-11-17 · 2 min read

Ray Kaminski, 58, of Canyon Country, was quoted $5,800 to insure the house in the canyon, which he cannot afford, so he cancelled the policy. Three thousand and fifty-seven dollars a year is the national average; the state pool raised rates 29.1 percent, taking a fire-zone premium to $5,800. It was once unbearable. It is not now. The premium is the climate's invoice, and the climate is the only landlord who has ever been honest about why it ruins you.

The numbers: $3,057 is less than a monthly payment on a mid-range German sedan, roughly a family-office managing partner's weekly lunch. For the median household it is nearly 5 percent of pre-tax income; in California's fire corridors, 9 percent from January 1. An associate had to run that.

"We insure the house only insofar as the owner acknowledges it stands where the climate has decided it does not want houses," said Carla Reyes-Eastman, the pool's chief actuary.

A different framing. Ray should ask whether he needs a house in a canyon. The market is not punishing anyone; it is clarifying. If the price of a roof exceeds the price of a handbag, maybe the roof is the problem, a structure so proud it prices itself out of its own ward. People who cannot afford the insurance cannot afford the house, which is a way of saying they cannot afford the climate, which is true. The rest is squeamishness.

“My insurance went up $3,057 this year but that's the cost of living in a place people want to be. If you can't afford the premium you can't afford the house. That's not the climate — that's the market.”

— Ray Kaminski
