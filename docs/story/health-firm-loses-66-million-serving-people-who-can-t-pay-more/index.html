Health Firm Loses $66 Million Serving People Who Can't Pay More

A company built on affordable healthcare reports record subscribers and a widening loss, because every patient it helps on the cheap tier costs it money. The mission is working. The numbers agree it is working.

TNTessa Nguyen
Economy & Data Reporter 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

Solace Health reported quarterly results that contained two internally consistent but contradictory numbers: subscription revenue grew 49 percent to $890 million, while the company lost $66.1 million on the quarter. More people are using the product than ever. Each additional subscriber on the company’s cheaper tier widens the loss. The enterprise operates on a gradient: a customer who can afford healthcare generates a profit; a customer who cannot afford healthcare subscribes to a lower-priced option and generates a loss. The more successfully the company fulfills its stated mission of affordable access, the more of the second type of customer it attracts, and the worse the quarterly report becomes.

The gross margin fell from 74 percent to 57 percent in a single quarter. For every dollar a customer pays, the company now spends seventeen cents more than it used to. The business model, as executed, is one in which serving additional people directly reduces profitability.

“My premium went up seventeen percent but they're serving more people than ever. If they lose money on every patient that means they're doing the right thing. Healthcare shouldn't be profitable.”

— a Solace Health subscriber, 62, who has never filed a claim

The stock has fallen more than 40 percent since the beginning of the year, a decline the market attributes to the difficulty of valuing a company that loses money the more people it helps. The company describes the dynamic as “scaling unprofitability.” A customer whose monthly budget for healthcare is $19 is not a cost center but, in the company’s framing, a statement of mission.

Management considered adjusting the product mix to market only to customers who can pay enough to generate a margin, a move that would improve the financial statements and render the enterprise’s stated reason for existing inoperative. The board declined. The company has instead chosen to continue serving the cohort that costs more than it pays, to describe the resulting losses as “expanding access,” and to allocate a dedicated line item for the shortfall.

At press time, Solace Health had enrolled a further 400,000 subscribers onto its most affordable tier, all of whom would cost the company more than they paid, and had described the quarter as “our most successful accessibility quarter ever,” with the share price down another 2 percent in acknowledgment.
