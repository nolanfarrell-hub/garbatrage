Overdraft Revenue Reaches $12 Billion, Funding the Very Hall of Shame That Documents It

A new report names the largest fee collectors while collecting them into a bracket, which is the last place the money will not be touched.

CR Claire Reyes
Debt & Fixed Income 2026-11-12 · 2 min read

Overdraft and non-sufficient-fund fees rose to a combined $12 billion, and the nation's leading consumer advocates responded by publishing an annual Hall of Shame, establishing the only institution that loses money on poverty twice.

The report found that a quarter of households pay overdraft fees each year, and that the largest banks continue to charge roughly $35 per overdraft, a figure that remains remarkably stable even as the balance it is charged on tends toward zero.

Critics note the fee exceeds the interest on most short-term loans, an observation the industry has rejected on the grounds that the overdraft is not a loan, it is a courtesy, and a courtesy that costs $35 is the closest thing the poor get to being a preferred customer.

Advocates called the report a victory in that it finally explained the fees, and the fee collectors a loss in that they now know they are being watched and have concluded the watching is content.
