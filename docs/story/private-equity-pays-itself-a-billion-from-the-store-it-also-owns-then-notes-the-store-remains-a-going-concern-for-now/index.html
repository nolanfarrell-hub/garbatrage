Private Equity Pays Itself A Billion From The Grocery Store, Replaces The Register Lady Who Knows Mrs. Eldridge's Bananas With A Kiosk That Does Not

A grocery chain's owners extracted a dividend funded by the company's own balance sheet while the chain announced another round of cuts, explaining that the money must go somewhere, and the somewhere was them.

EMElena Marsh
Head of Research 2026-11-11 · 2 min read

Denise Okonkwo works register 4 at the Piggly Wiggly on Highway 49. She has been at register 4 for 11 years. She knows the regulars by their produce choices. She knows that Mrs. Eldridge buys bananas one at a time and that Mr. Vest will ask for a price check on the store-brand ketchup even though he has asked for a price check on the store-brand ketchup every Tuesday since 2019 and it has always been $1.89. Last week the store installed a kiosk where Denise used to stand. The kiosk does not ask for price checks. The kiosk does not know Mrs. Eldridge's bananas. The kiosk is owned by the same company that owns the dividend.

Buoyant Capital, the firm that owns the chain, paid itself a $1.1 billion dividend in October. The money came from the chain. The chain got the money from the people who buy the bananas. Denise got a notice that her hours are being cut to 24 a week. “The notice was on the bulletin board next to a picture of the new kiosk,” Denise said. “The kiosk does not have hours. The kiosk is never tired. The kiosk has never needed a price check on ketchup that costs $1.89. The kiosk is the best employee we have ever had.”

A clerk who stayed past her cut hours explained the dividend as a “return on strategy” and the hour cuts as “a natural evolution of the labor model.” When asked what the strategy was, he said owning the store. When asked what the natural evolution was, he said fewer people. When asked if the dividend could have funded the people, he said the dividend was funded by the store, and the store is the people, and the people are the hours, and the hours were the dividend, and the dividend was him.

Denise now works 24 hours a week. The kiosk works 24 hours a day. The kiosk does not have a 401(k). The kiosk does not have a daughter studying nursing at the community college. The kiosk has a reset button. “I asked management if the kiosk is my replacement,” Denise said. “They said the kiosk is not my replacement. They said the kiosk is my colleague. I asked what the colleague earns. They said the colleague does not earn anything. I said then the colleague is not a colleague. They said the colleague is a capital expenditure. I said does the capital expenditure need a price check on the ketchup. They said the capital expenditure knows the price of the ketchup. I asked what the price is. They said $1.89. I said thank you.”

“Denise told Mr. Vest the kiosk knows the price of the ketchup is $1.89. I have been in that aisle for 11 years and the kiosk is the most dignified thing in it because the kiosk does not need a price check and the kiosk does not ask where I have been.”

— Denise, who watched 11 years of ketchup
