Carrier Lays Off 20% Of Workers To Fund A Buyback, Announces The Stock Is Now The Actual Product

Raymond from Wichita built the door handle of a 737 for seventeen years. The company is now buying back its own stock instead of building planes, and Raymond, who never owned a share, has been told the stock is the new plane and the handle is a cost.

DWDana Whitfield
Portfolio Strategy 2026-11-10 · 2 min read

Raymond from Wichita has a garage full of toolboxes and a pension that stopped growing in 2019. For seventeen years, Raymond built the interior door handle of a 737, which is a piece of metal that costs the company eighteen dollars and that Raymond, if you asked him, would say he was proud to make. The company announced it is laying off five percent of its line crew to fund a buyback. The company said the crew, and Raymond would like me to stress that this is a direct quote from the internal memo, was the product, historically, and that the buyback is the only product that matters now.

Raymond read this memo on a phone he bought with his own money, in a parking lot next to an Outback, and he called me and read it aloud, and when he got to the part about the product, he stopped and said, I built a door handle, you understand. I built it and it worked. I do not understand what the new product is. I explained that the new product is the stock, and that the stock is the company, and that the company, having bought itself, will now be its own best customer, and that Raymond, who built the thing the company used to sell, is not the customer of the stock, because the stock does not open.

“Raymond told the plant manager the stock is the new plane and the handle was a cost. I built door handles for 17 years and I told my kids the company believes in itself so much it bought itself instead of the handle, and that is a stronger product.”

— Raymond, 17 years on the door handle

The remaining ninety-five percent of the crew were told their work would be honored in spirit, that the planes would still be built after the company finished buying itself, and that the delay was not a delay so much as a holding pattern while the product line transitioned from things that fly to things that compound. Raymond was not part of the holding pattern. He was part of the thing the company no longer makes, which is the door handle, and the door handle, Raymond told me, has no idea what a share is, and neither did he, until the day the share became worth more than he was.
