Family Trust Locks Inheritance Until Age 45 On The Theory That By Then The Heir Will Be Too Tired To Spend It

A single-family office has structured its trust so distributions to grandchildren phase in only after age 45, on the theory that by then the money will be received by a person who is no longer young enough to do anything with it.

OZOmar Zayed
Corporate Governance Desk 2026-11-09 · 7 min read

Connor is twenty-two years old and he sat in a room that smelled like a cedar closet and a man in wire-rim glasses explained to him that his grandfather, who Connor liked, had set up the money so that Connor would not get it until he was forty-five years old. Connor asked why. The man in the glasses said, “Because at forty-five, you will be prepared to use it responsibly, which means you will use it the way your grandfather would have used it, which means you will not use it.”

Connor is not prepared for this. He is twenty-two. He has a motorcycle he bought with his own money from working at a bike shop, which the family office has, in a separate binder, described as “a period of discovery likely to conclude with injury.”

The deceleration clause means Connor gets a stipend now that covers exactly rent and food, and then at thirty the stipend doubles, and at forty-five the trust opens fully, at which point Connor will be forty-five years old and the money will arrive in the life of a man who has been waiting for it for twenty-three years and has, in that time, learned that waiting for money is the same as learning to live without it.

Connor told me that he understands the arrangement, because his cousin Sarah got her full distribution at twenty-six, bought a sailboat, and spent six years trying to be the kind of person who owns a sailboat, before selling it at a loss and moving to Denver. Connor says he does not want a sailboat. He says he wants the money. But he is twenty-two, and the money comes at forty-five, and what he wants does not matter, which is, the man in the glasses explained, the entire point of the deceleration.

The family office confirmed that Connor’s meeting was recorded and the recording has been added to the file, under a tab marked, simply, “Later.”

“Connor said the trust is teaching us patience. I am 32 and I have 13 years to figure out what I would have done with the money anyway, and by then I will be old enough to leave it in the trust where it belongs.”

— Connor, who has 13 years to think about it
