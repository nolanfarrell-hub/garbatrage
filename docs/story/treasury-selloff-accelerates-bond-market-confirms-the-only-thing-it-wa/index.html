Treasury Selloff Accelerates as Bond Market Learns the Worst Part of Being Right Is Having Been Right the Whole Time

With the 10-year toward 5.1% and hawkish Fed bets rising, the long end reports that vindication, apparently, hurts.

TN Tessa Nguyen
Economy & Data Reporter 2026-11-14 · 2 min read

Gerald Twine has been telling anyone at his firm who will listen that rates are going higher for eighteen months. He has said it in meetings. He has said it in the hallway. He has said it to the guy who brings the sandwich cart, who told Gerald he did not trade bonds and could not care less about the long end of the curve. Gerald said the long end cared about him.

On Friday the 10-year yield pushed toward 5.10 percent, and Gerald felt the specific loneliness of being right about something terrible. He called his wife to tell her. She asked if that meant the bonus would be bigger. Gerald said it did not. She asked what it meant. Gerald said it meant he had been right. She said that was not a thing you could spend.

The selloff accelerated after sticky PCE data and hawkish Fed language, confirming what Gerald had been saying since the summer of last year, which was that inflation was not going anywhere and the market was pricing in a fairy tale. The market, now pricing in a selloff instead, confirmed that being right had been its goal all along, and that the goal was somehow worse than being wrong, because being wrong came with a lower cost of living.

"I told my wife I was vindicated. She said vindicated did not show up in the checking account. I told her it showed up in the history books. She said the history books were not the ones buying the groceries." — Gerald Twine, Duration Manager

At press time, Gerald was still at his desk at 7 p.m., staring at a screen that confirmed what he had been saying for a year and a half, which nobody was celebrating, least of all Gerald, who had been right and had nothing to show for it except a spreadsheet and a wife who had stopped asking about the bonus.
