Rich Guy Locks Up His Money for 900 Years After His Granddaughter Asks for Bus Fare

Ted Barlow sits his family down at a Chili's in Boca Raton to explain, over lukewarm quesadillas, that the money will be available to them in roughly thirty generations, provided the trust's actuarial committee still considers them descendants.

OZ Omar Zayed
Estate & Governance Desk · Trusts 2026-11-05 · 2 min read

Let me tell you about Ted Barlow. Ted is seventy-two. Ted has a lot of money. Ted's granddaughter, whose name he sometimes remembers, asked him for bus fare last Tuesday. Not a down payment on a house. Bus fare. Twelve dollars and forty cents.

Ted thought about it all night. He thought about it the way a man thinks about a rat he saw in the kitchen. The rat is not the problem. The rat is a symptom. The granddaughter asking for bus fare means the granddaughter is in the world, and the world is where bus fare comes from, and Ted has spent his entire life making sure his money never goes anywhere near the world.

So Ted called his lawyer. The lawyer, a man named Stuart who has the energy of a funeral director who just got a promotion, spent four hundred hours designing a trust that would last nine hundred years. The document is seven hundred pages. Page three is a list of things the money cannot be used for, and the first thing on the list is bus fare. The second thing is grandchildren.

“The trust will outlive my granddaughter by about 870 years. A piece of paper that lasts longer than a person — that is commitment.”

— Ted, at a Chili's in Boca Raton

The trust is structured to survive wars, depressions, climate collapse, and the extinction of the Barlow family itself. If the last Barlow dies without children, the money goes to a university that Ted has never visited, on the condition that it never name a building after him. "I don't want my name on anything," Ted told Stuart. "I want my name on the account statement, and I want the account statement to be the only thing anyone remembers me by."

At the signing ceremony, held in a conference room that smelled like old carpet and newer regret, Ted's granddaughter asked if she could at least have the pen. Stuart said the pen was an asset of the trust, and that the trust's distribution schedule did not include stationery until year 312. The granddaughter asked what happens in year 312. Stuart said the trust would purchase a new box of pens and distribute them to the descendants, provided the descendants could still write.

Ted described the trust as a gift to his family. "It means none of them will ever have to work," he said. "It also means none of them will ever have the money to do anything else, which is, I think, the balance we were looking for."
