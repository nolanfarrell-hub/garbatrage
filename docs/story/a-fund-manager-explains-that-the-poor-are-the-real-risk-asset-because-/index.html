Fund Manager Calls Working Poor the Highest-Beta Asset, Wife Who Works Two Jobs Asks What Beta Is

Kevin Tran manages $4 billion. His wife Maria manages two tables at a diner. Kevin explained to Maria that her income is volatile, that this volatility is "beautiful from a portfolio standpoint," and that he has no idea how much a gallon of milk costs.

OZ Omar Zayed
Corporate Governance Desk 2026-11-06 · 2 min read

Kevin Tran manages money. He manages a lot of money. He manages it well enough that he has an office with a door and a title that says "portfolio manager" and a charter that says he can call things beta. His wife Maria manages a section at a diner called The Skillet. Maria does not have a door. Maria does not have a title. Maria does not have beta.

Kevin came home one night and told Maria about his fund's new highest-conviction position. "The working poor," Kevin said. "They are the highest-beta asset on the board. They go up when the economy breathes and down when it coughs. It is the most beautiful volatility profile I have ever seen."

“He said my income is volatile and that is beautiful from a portfolio standpoint. I asked if beta paid for our daughter's shoes — he said that is not what beta is for.”

— Maria, who works two jobs and asked what beta is

Kevin's model treats low-wage workers as an instrument. Their wages rise when the economy is good, which makes them a growth asset. Their wages fall when the economy is bad, which makes them a value trap. "The asymmetry is perfect," Kevin said. "They do all the moving. I do all the measuring. It is a beautiful partnership."

Maria went back to work the next morning. She carried the trays. She dealt with the customers. She made the tips. Kevin checked the model. The model said the working poor were up. Kevin was pleased. He did not think about where the up came from. He did not need to. The model did not ask. The model does not ask anything. The model is like Kevin in that way.

"She is the asset," Kevin said. "I am the allocator. The asset does not need to understand the allocation. The asset just needs to keep being an asset, which she is, reliably, every morning at six."
