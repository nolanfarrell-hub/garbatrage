A Fund Manager Explains That The Poor Are The Real Risk Asset, Because They Keep Going Up, And Then, Irritatingly, Down

The manager, outlining allocations, identified the working poor as the fund's highest-beta exposure, citing the way their lot improves with every quarter of wage growth, then deteriorates with every quarter of anything else, and has established a dedicated sleeve to trade the whiplash, which he called, without irony, the human hedge.

OZ Omar Zayed
Corporate Governance Desk 2026-11-06 · 2 min read

Let me walk you through the logic, because it is a logic, and it is airtight, and that is what makes it maddening. A portfolio manager, explaining his book, has identified the working poor as the single highest-beta asset class he trades, and he means it as a compliment, sort of.

See, here is the thing, and you are going to want to sit down, because it is structural: low-income workers go up every time the economy throws them a crumb, which makes them, in back-testing, a delightful growth story. And they go down every time the economy takes the crumb back, which, historically, is almost immediately. Put that together, and you have what the manager calls the human hedge: an asset that reliably rises to the bait and reliably re-rates lower, providing, he explains, both a buy and a sell signal in the same calendar year.

He is not even being mean about it. He is being precise. He is reading their lives the way he reads an earnings transcript, for the beta, and the beta, in this case, is that their lot improves and deteriorates with a correlation to the business cycle that would make a quant weep with gratitude. They are, to his mind, the ideal instrument, because they do not even need to be told what happened. They just feel it, and re-rate, and go back to work.

“We are not exploiting a population. We are calibrating to a yield. The fact that the yield is a person's entire wage is, and I say this with tremendous respect for the complexity involved, not something the model has been asked to hold.”

The manager concluded his investor call with a note of genuine admiration, telling the room that the working poor are the most responsive economic input he has ever run, that they adjust faster than any index constituent, and that if they could only stop complaining, they would be the perfect instrument. The room applauded. The work, presumably, got quieter.
