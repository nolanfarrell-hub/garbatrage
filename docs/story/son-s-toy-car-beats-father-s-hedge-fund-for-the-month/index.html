Son's Toy Car Beats Father's Hedge Fund for the Month

At the birthday party, he did the math on a die-cast car and now nobody can get him off the patio.

MRMarcus Reid
Markets Anchor · NYSE Floor 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

Somewhere in Greenwich, a private-equity father is standing on his own patio at his son's eighth birthday party, holding a die-cast car between his thumb and forefinger like it's a piece of evidence in a fraud trial. The numbers do not make sense to him. He has watched the toy pile accumulate all afternoon — the same $800 star-cruiser-class replica his son gets every year, the same remote-control thing that will be under the couch by Tuesday — and he has never, in forty-three years of consuming goods, once looked at a toy price. Toys were a category that existed below his attention, like lint or municipal bus schedules. But then he picked up the orange one. A 1995 Mazda RX-7, die-cast, one-inch long. The Bureau of Labor Statistics says toy prices rose 4.2% in a single month, the largest monthly increase in the index's entire 49-year history, and this specific car, this tiny orange piece of painted metal that a child will lose in a sofa cushion within 72 hours, went up more in thirty days than his entire portfolio dropped in the worst trading week of the year. He has not stopped talking about it. The party magician is doing balloon animals to an empty tent.

“My son's die-cast car went up more in a month than my 401k did all year. That's not a toy — that's a better asset than anything my advisor put me in and I'm buying the whole set.”

— a dad at a birthday party, 39, who is now shopping for display cases

He has now asked his assistant to pull the supply chain on the die-cast line. She is on hold with Apex Die-Cast's vendor relations line. The children are eating cake off a table that cost more than most people's first cars, and Thaddeus is explaining the difference between PPI and CPI to a seven-year-old who just wants to know if the orange car can go through a loop. At press time, Shoreline Equity was reportedly evaluating a bid for the entire die-cast vehicle sector, which Thaddeus described as 'the only asset class left that still has upside.'
