Telecom Confident Its Own Shares Are Worth $19.45, No, Really, They Are

When the bidder's own stock at its own closing price is used to calculate the offer's value, and the market says the math is wrong, finance says the market is wrong.

PSPriya Sharma
Washington Bureau, Fed & Rates, plus household costs (insurance, mortgages) 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

A $31 billion takeover offer can be valued in two ways: by the bidder, using its own stock price, and by the market, using the same stock price and arriving at a different number. NorthPeak Communications, a midsize telecom operator based in Montreal, is offering $6.35 in cash plus 0.17 of a NorthPeak Class B share for each share of Westland Telecom, a larger Vancouver-based rival. NorthPeak values the package at $19.45 per share, calculated using its own closing price as of the Friday before the announcement. Westland’s stock closed at $12.14 on that same Friday.

The difference between $19.45 and $12.14 is $7.31 per share, or roughly $11.7 billion in implied value that the bidder claims exists and the market says does not. The gap arises from the Class B share component: NorthPeak is offering its own stock at a price it determined by referencing its own stock price, then assigning that fraction a fixed value. By the time the deal closes, the bidder’s stock will have moved. The only guaranteed element is the $6.35 in cash.

“The stock is $19.45 and the market says it's worth less but I trust the company. They know their own value better than a trading algorithm. I bought at $19.45 and I'm holding.”

— a retail investor, 55, who bought the dip

The offer represents a 60 percent premium to Westland’s closing price under the bidder’s arithmetic. Under the market’s arithmetic, the actual consideration is $6.35 plus whatever the Class B shares are worth on the day they arrive, a variable the bidder does not control. The $11.7 billion difference between the two math systems is value the bidder has assigned to a number that may change before the transaction completes.

NorthPeak shareholders will vote on the share issuance in December. Westland shareholders will vote on the deal in January. Both will be presented with the $19.45 figure.

At press time, NorthPeak’s Class B shares had not been re-priced to account for the market’s disagreement. The company said it stood by its valuation methodology.
