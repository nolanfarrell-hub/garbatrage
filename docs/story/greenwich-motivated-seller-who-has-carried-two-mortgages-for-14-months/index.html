Greenwich Motivated Seller Who Has Carried Two Mortgages for 14 Months Begins Accepting Applications for the Role of Buyer

Existing home sales hit a 16-year low of 3.75 million annualized in October. Every price tier declined, including the $1-million-and-up segment. In Greenwich, Connecticut, a Colonial has been on the market for 14 months.

PSPriya Sharma
Washington Bureau, Fed & Rates, plus household costs (insurance, mortgages) 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

Gerald Masterson laminated a piece of printer paper and taped it to the wrought-iron railing of his front porch at 47 Maple Lane. "Yes, I Know It's Been 14 Months," the FAQ read, in 14-point Helvetica. "Question Two: Why Is It Still on the Market? Answer: I Have Been Carrying Two Mortgages Since September 2025 and Am Open to Creative Financing Solutions. Question Three: Define Creative. Answer: Non-Contingent Offer Plus Assumption of Both the Primary Mortgage and the Bridge Loan, Closing Within 30 Days, No Home Inspection, No Appraisal Contingency, Buyer to Cover Transfer Tax, Seller's Attorney Fees, and the Remaining Balance of the Lawn Service Contract Through April 2027." The lawn service had not been paid since August. The grass was 14 inches tall.

Masterson's listing has become a local attraction. Teenagers from the nearby high school photograph the sign as it evolves. The first sign read "For Sale." The second, in month four, read "Price Reduced." The third, in month seven, read "Will Consider Offers That Are Not Actively Insulting." The fourth, in month eleven, read "Open House — Serious Inquiries Only, Definition of Serious Negotiable." The current sign, installed last week, reads "Accepting Applications for the Role of Buyer" and includes a QR code linking to a seven-page application form that asks for the applicant's "relationship to real estate (please describe in 500 words or fewer)," "three professional references who can attest to your ability to close a transaction," and "a brief statement on why you believe you are the right person to assume ownership of this property at this time." The application does not ask for an offer amount. "If they're filling out the application, they're serious," Masterson said. "The offer is a formality. The application is the screen."

Masterson has also petitioned the Greenwich Planning and Zoning Commission to designate 47 Maple Lane a local landmark — a status that would require the town to maintain the property's exterior and lawn in perpetuity. The petition argues that the house "represents a significant example of early-2000s neo-Colonial architecture and the single-family-home seller's psychological arc from confident list price to desperate non-contingency." If approved, the Greenwich Preservation Trust would inherit the lawn-mowing, the roof maintenance, and the interior mold that has spread across the master bathroom ceiling since Masterson stopped paying the electric bill in August. "It's not a loss," Masterson said. "It's a public-private partnership. The town gets a landmark. I get someone else to cut the grass. The buyer gets a property with historic-tax advantages. Everyone wins." The mold was not mentioned in the landmark application.

“I've carried two mortgages for fourteen months and the bank approved both. If they didn't think I could handle it they wouldn't have given me the loans. The second property is an investment.”

— a homeowner, 52, who has refinanced twice this year

At press time, Masterson had received four applications for the role of buyer, rejected all four for insufficient answers to the essay question, and was preparing a seventh sign: "Landmark Designation Pending — Inquire Within About the Museum of My Own Poor Timing, Admission = One Non-Contingent Offer at 85% of Asking, No Lowballers, I Know What I Have."
