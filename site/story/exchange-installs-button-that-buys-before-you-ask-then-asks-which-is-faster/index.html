Exchange Installs Button That Buys Before You Tap It, Then Charges You For Being The Part Of The System That Is Slowest

Denise from Tucson tried to buy one share of Ford on her phone. Before she could tap the button, a machine in New Jersey had bought the share, sold it to her for a penny more, and charged her for the speed of her own slowness. The exchange says the button is a co-located assist and that Denise, being human, is the latency.

DWDana Whitfield
Portfolio Strategy 2026-11-11 · 2 min read

Denise from Tucson sat on her couch with her phone in her hand and a single share of Ford Motor Company in her brain and she tapped the green button on the brokerage app and the button did nothing, because by the time her thumb reached the glass, a machine in a building in New Jersey that is exactly one hundred and twenty feet closer to the exchange than her phone will ever be had already done what Denise was about to do, and had done it faster, and had charged Denise a fraction of a cent for the privilege of arriving second, and the fraction of a cent, multiplied by every person who has ever tapped a button, is the entire business model of the button that taps itself before you tap it.

The exchange calls the feature a co-located assist, and what it does is press itself, because the hand that wanted to press it is still thirty nanoseconds behind, which is the entire career arc of the average retail investor. Denise asked the customer service person, a woman named Priya who was very patient, whether she had actually bought the share or whether the machine had bought the share and then sold it to her. Priya said both were true. The machine bought it. Denise bought it from the machine. The machine made one tenth of one cent. Denise owned one share of Ford. Everyone was happy.

“Denise told Priya the speed is the price of being a person. The machine bought the share before I could tap the button and the machine sold it to me for 0.1 cents, and I told Denise the 0.1 cents is the cost of being slower than New Jersey.”

— Denise, slower than New Jersey

The exchange described the arrangement as offering everyone the same access, which is true, in the way that everyone has the same view of a hallway that is already filled with faster people. Denise's order arrived. It was just that by the time it arrived, it was the answer to someone else's question, and the question was how much Denise would pay to be the answer to her own question, which was a question Denise had not asked, because the machine had already answered it.
