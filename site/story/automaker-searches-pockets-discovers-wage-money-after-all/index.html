Automaker Searches Pockets, Discovers Wage Money After All

The auto-parts supplier told its union it could not afford their demands. The same week it started permanent layoffs, its board approved a $60 million stock buyback and a record $46 million dividend.

DWDana Whitfield
Labor & Industry Correspondent 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

The arithmetic of corporate allocation is a matter of column assignment. In June, workers at Meridian Automotive’s Bristol, Michigan plant requested higher wages. The company said it could not afford them, a position it repeated in bargaining sessions. An 86 percent strike-authorization vote did not alter the response. The union, acting on the company’s representations about its financial condition, did not call a strike. Workers accepted a contract that gave the company substantially what it wanted. “We are told they are losing money and if we didn’t accept, they would just pull out,” one worker summarized at the time.

On November 11, the Meridian board approved the company’s first-ever share repurchase program, up to $60 million, to be executed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange. The buyback follows a July dividend of approximately $46 million, which the company described as “the highest dividend payout ratio in the Company’s history.” Together, the two payouts direct more than $106 million to shareholders. The announcement arrived the same week the company acknowledged it had started permanent layoffs at the Bristol plant.

“They announced a $60 million buyback the same week they laid people off and I support it. The shareholders are the owners. The layoffs are efficiency — that's how the company stays competitive.”

— a former Bristol plant worker, 48, who still owns company shares

The company did not misrepresent its financial position. The $106 million was allocated to the shareholder returns column, not the wages column. In August, Meridian sold roughly 1.8 million square feet of industrial property, including Plants 1, 5, 6 and 7, to a Milwaukee-based real estate firm. It retained Plants 3 and 4 and leased back portions of the sold properties during what it described as a “planned transition.”

Workers report that production is now being concentrated in the retained plants, with employees holding three years or less seniority — the newest hires, the same cohort the union reportedly instructed to vote yes — first in line for the cuts. The company has declined to specify how many workers are being laid off. Those who lose their jobs may return as part-timers at $27 per hour, capped at 27 to 29 hours per week, with no company health coverage, no paid time off and no sick leave.

At press time, Meridian’s stock was up 2.4 percent on the buyback announcement, and the company confirmed it remained in a strong liquidity position to address any future claims on its capital.
