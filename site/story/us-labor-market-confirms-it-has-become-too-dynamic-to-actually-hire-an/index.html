Fed Worried the Labor Market Has Gotten Too Settled to Count Anyone As Working

Federal Reserve officials are worried the labor market has gotten so settled that a person can no longer be counted as hired, laid off, or doing anything at all.

TNTessa Nguyen
Economy & Data Reporter 2026-11-14 · 2 min read

Greg Furnish has not been fired in eleven months, and the Federal Reserve has noticed. Greg works in a warehouse on the south side of Richmond. He stacks boxes that come off a truck and stacks them onto a pallet. The boxes are the same shape every day. The amount he gets paid for stacking them has not changed in fourteen months. Greg considers this a good run.

At the Fed's last meeting, officials reviewed the labor market data and identified Greg's life as a problem. Hiring was low. Layoffs were low. Nobody was quitting and nobody was being hired, which meant the labor market had achieved what economists called full settlement, a state in which a person is neither advancing nor retreating, merely existing in the same job at the same wage while the central bank watches and wonders whether this counts as employment.

Greg, for his part, did not know he was being studied. He drove the same route to the warehouse every morning, parked in the same spot, and stacked the same boxes. When he heard the Fed was worried about the labor market being too settled, he asked his supervisor what that meant. His supervisor said it meant the Fed thought people should be moving around more. Greg asked where they were supposed to move to. His supervisor said he did not know, but presumably somewhere else.

“My supervisor said the Fed thought I should be moving around more, and I asked him where I was supposed to move to, and he said he did not know but presumably somewhere else, and I told him that was good enough for me because somewhere else was where opportunity was.”

— a warehouse associate who is open to relocation

At press time, Greg had been offered no better jobs, had applied for none, and planned to come back to the warehouse tomorrow, which the Fed said was fine for now, but was not the same as improvement.
