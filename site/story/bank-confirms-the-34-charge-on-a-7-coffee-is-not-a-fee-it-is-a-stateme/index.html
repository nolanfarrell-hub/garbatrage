Bank Confirms The $34 Charge On A $7 Coffee Is Not A Fee But A Statement, Emphatically Made, At 7:04 A.M., Without Danielle From Tulsa's Permission

Danielle from Tulsa bought a $6.75 latte at 6:58 AM with $21 in her account. By 7:04, the latte was $40.75 and Danielle had been charged a courtesy the bank now says was not a fee at all, but a statement, emphatically made, on her behalf, without her permission.

CR Claire Reyes
Debt & Fixed Income 2026-11-11 · 2 min read

Danielle from Tulsa bought a six-dollar-and-seventy-five-cent latte at six-fifty-eight in the morning, which is sixteen minutes before the bank opens and seventeen dollars and twenty-five cents more than she needed to not be late for the shift at the dental office where she answers phones. At seven-oh-four, the bank had turned the latte into forty dollars and seventy-five cents and charged Danielle a courtesy she did not ask for and a message she did not consent to, and when Danielle called to ask why, the bank said the charge was not a fee. The bank said it was a statement. A statement somebody should be making, the bank said, and since Danielle was not making it, the bank made it for her, out of the money she did not have.

Danielle asked what the statement was. The bank said the statement was that Danielle had spent money she did not own at a time when the bank was not there to ask, and that the statement, now that it had been made, would be delivered to Danielle in the form of a thirty-four-dollar addition to her latte, which Danielle could now read, every time she looked at her balance, as a text the bank had written, in fees, because the bank did not have her phone number.

“Danielle told the bank the $34 carried more weight than a text. I read the statement every time I check my balance and I know the bank wrote it at 7:04 A.M. without my permission, and I told Danielle the permission is the $34 and the $34 is the statement.”

— Danielle, who read the statement at 7:04

The bank reiterated that nobody is ever charged for being poor, and that the thirty-four dollars was not a charge for poverty but a charge for the statement, and that the statement, having been made, would remain on Danielle's account as a permanent record of the bank's commitment to saying what Danielle was unwilling to say herself, which was that she was seventeen dollars and twenty-five cents away from a latte she had already drunk.
