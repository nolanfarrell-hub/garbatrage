Denise Put The Orange Juice Back Because It Was Eight Dollars, And Core Inflation Did Not Count It

October consumer-price data due Tuesday will decide whether the December hike gets cemented, but analysts already frame 2026 as the year core inflation declined to admit defeat slowly — a period households are believed to have spent in a state economists describe as 'normal now.'

TN Tessa Nguyen
Economy & Data Reporter 2026-11-09 · 7 min read

Denise is in the checkout line at the Fry’s in north Phoenix. She has a cart. The cart has chicken, broccoli, a bag of rice, milk, and the orange juice that she is currently putting back, because the orange juice is now eight dollars, and she has already put the orange juice back three times this year, and she knows the number now.

Tuesday, the Bureau of Labor Statistics is going to announce that core inflation held at whatever number it held at, and the number will not have orange juice in it, because core inflation is inflation without the things that move, meaning without the things that move. Orange juice moves. Gas moves. Your apartment moves. The number that decides if the Fed raises rates again is a number that has delicately removed the part of your life where stuff gets more expensive every month.

Denise has never heard of core CPI, which is good, because if she had heard of it, she would have to hear that it is the inflation number that matters, and she would have to hear that it is doing fine, and she would have to decide whether to be glad that her problem has been successfully removed from the measurement.

Her son, Marcus, is in the cart. Marcus is two. He does not know what inflation is. He knows the orange juice was on the belt and now it is not. He is not happy about it. Denise tells him it is not the orange juice that matters. She is right, but only in the way that a person who has run out of wrong things to say is right.

Tuesday, the Fed will look at the core number, and if it goes up, they will raise rates in December, which makes everything more expensive, including the things that core does not count. Marcus will not understand that either. He will just notice that the orange juice is being put back more often now, and he will start to accept it, which is how acceptance works under a system that has structured itself to be accepted.

“Core inflation does not count orange juice. That is a feature, not a bug. If the number that matters does not count the thing that hurts, then the thing that hurts is not the thing that matters. Denise put the orange juice back. The number stayed the same. The system worked.”

— a shopper who does not track core inflation but trusts it
