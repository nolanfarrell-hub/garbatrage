Machines That Cost a Country's GDP Still Can't Book a Table

$106 billion in a quarter. It still can't get a table at eight.

DWDana Whitfield
Portfolio Strategy 2026-11-17 · 2 min read

Dierdre Baines, 45, a ratepayer outside Ashburn, is on the hook, and the hook is the demand charge on the residential grid. The seven firms that run the world's computing backbone spent $106 billion on AI infrastructure in the third quarter. Full-year: $690 billion, more than the GDP of Switzerland, Sweden and Belgium combined, roughly the U.S. Medicare budget, on machines that do not yet book a restaurant table without inventing a fake confirmation number.

"The spending is magnificent," said Leonard Torrance of Westwater Capital. "These companies are building the computational equivalent of the interstate highway system."

The side effects are already visible: 460 terawatt-hours this year, 12 percent of national generation, up from 4 percent. Northern Virginia's utility admits it cannot guarantee residential reliability at peak; out West the cooling has drained aquifers. Ratepayers absorb the demand charge.

As it stands, the grid is about to be taken from behind by machines that cannot feed themselves, and the ratepayer is the one holding the lube.

ELECTIONS: One party loses, the other does not win. COMMODITIES: Coffee futures rose. Something about Brazil. ENERGY: Natural gas is up. People are cold. Data centers are not, and they have the grid by the throat.

“The data centers use a lot of power but that's the price of progress. I'll make that trade.”

— Dierdre Baines
