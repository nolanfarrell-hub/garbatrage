Average 30-Year Rate Reaches 7.4%, Affordable Home Now A Family Heirloom To Be Passed Down Unopened

With the average mortgage rate at a three-year high, the industry has confirmed the starter home is no longer bought, only inherited.

OZ Omar Zayed
Markets Reporter 2026-11-14 · 2 min read

AUSTIN, TX—The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate rose to 7.4% this month, its highest in three years, prompting the real-estate industry to formally reclassify the affordable home from a purchase into a generational heirloom, to be passed down unopened and, ideally, still occupied by someone with a lower rate.

Local title companies have begun selling a companion product called a provenance rider, which for a modest closing fee establishes that the home has always been in the family, even when, as is often the case, the family is currently living in one of the outbuildings. Appraisers have retrained accordingly, valuing homes not on square footage but on how many generations have so far failed to die inside them.

“We stopped asking buyers whether they can afford the house and started asking them which child deserves to inherit the regret,” said loan officer Marcus Webb, whose own mother was reported to be holding the deed to a starter home in a trust that will release it upon proof that nobody has lived in it in forty years.

“I don’t owe anything on this house and I’m still losing to it. It just sits there, beating my children on appreciation.” — Delia Vasquez, Homeowner, addressing her own mortgage

First-time buyers remain welcome at the open houses, where agents have begun handing them laminated cards explaining that the home they are seeing is not for them but merely being exhibited to them, the way one exhibits a cathedral to a tourist who will never be Catholic. The tour concludes with a brief discussion of the estate tax.

At press time, the industry had announced its strongest product yet: a new 45-year mortgage designed, in the lender’s phrase, for a family that will not “even be born into a position to start dying inside it” until two more generations have had a chance to look at the house first.
