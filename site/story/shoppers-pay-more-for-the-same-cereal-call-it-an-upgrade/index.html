Shoppers Pay More for the Same Cereal, Call It an Upgrade

Food-at-home rose 0.4 percent in October; the department sees 2.3 percent for the year.

OZ Omar Zayed
Consumer & Earnings Desk · New York 2026-11-17 · 2 min read

Marta watched the pasta she buys climb to $3.49 and assumed it was better. Food-at-home rose 0.4 percent in October; the department sees 2.3 percent for the year. Harold Peavey remembers the moment he stopped worrying what a box of cereal costs. July 2023, the distributor raised a fourth time in eight months; then-operating chief Peavey told pricing to raise shelf price by the increase, plus twelve percent. He assumed customers would switch to the store brand. They did not.

"They just paid it," Peavey said. "That was the moment I realized we had been underpricing food for thirty years."

Today he is chief executive of Atlantic Grocers, a 340-store case study in what the industry calls 'pricing liberation.' His private-label pasta margin is 47 percent; in 2019 it was 22. Nothing changed about the pasta. He took a 24-pack of paper towels from $18.99 to $24.99, a 31 percent increase; unit sales dipped, recovered, then beat the old level. "The customer interpreted the higher price as a signal of quality," he explains, a sentence you could mistype, send to the board, and have signed into law. Gross margin is up 540 basis points; Peavey got a $4.2 million bonus.

"The grocery business is not a charity," he said. "If the customer decides the price is too high, they can eat at a restaurant." Marta strokes the bag of pasta and says nothing.

“They just paid it. That was the moment I realized we had been underpricing food for thirty years.”
