Card Issuers Raise Limits on Customers Already in Debt, Explain Strategy as 'Giving the Rope Time to Work'

With balances and minimums climbing, lenders say the best defense against default is a higher ceiling.

CR Claire Reyes
Consumer Finance Reporter 2026-11-14 · 2 min read

NEW YORK—The Federal Reserve's quarterly survey found that average credit-card limits increased during the third quarter even as card balances grew, prompting banks to explain the policy as an expression of confidence in a consumer whom the economy has spent the year gutting.

"We raise limits not because we think the customer can afford more but because we think they've earned the right to find out," said a consumer-credit executive who asked not to be named, adding that the industry calls the approach "relationship expansion," though a more precise term might be "hope underwriting."

Rico Espinoza, 31, a warehouse associate in Phoenix, said his bank raised his limit by $2,000 and sent a card letting him know it had arrived. "It was the only invitation I've gotten all year," Espinoza said. "Not a raise, not a better apartment, not a call back from the job I applied to. Just a piece of plastic saying I am approved to owe more. Yes, I am going. I'm going to use it. What else am I supposed to do with an invitation?"

"They raised my limit and then sent a survey asking why I used it. I wrote back: because you thought of me. Because you saw me. Because no one else sent a card. Because my electricity bill came and my checking account said no and the credit card said yes, and yes felt better than no, even if yes costs 24 percent." — Keisha Fowler, Certified Nursing Assistant, Tampa

At press time, the bank had reportedly followed up with a second survey asking why the customer had taken such an aggressive view of their own financial future, and the customer had replied that they had learned it from the bank itself.
