Terrence Checks His Phone At 4 A.M. To See If The ETF Has Gone Up On His Anxiety

The fund's mandate is to buy exactly what a shift worker fears at 4 a.m., then sell it at 5, then buy it back at 9, and it is outperforming the broader market because, the fund explains, anxiety is the one thing that is always in season.

PSPriya Sharma
Washington Bureau · Fed & Rates 2026-11-09 · 2 min read

Terrence works third shift at a logistics warehouse outside Memphis. He gets off at 6 a.m. He checks his phone at 4 a.m., during his break, to see if the schedule changed, because the schedule changes by text message, and if the text says his shift is cut, he loses the night’s pay, and he has already driven forty minutes to get there. At 4 a.m., Terrence is not hoping. He is waiting. He is waiting for the text that either lets him keep working or sends him home. He is the most reliable thing in the American economy.

There is an exchange‑traded fund that is priced on what Terrence feels at 4 a.m. The fund does not own Terrence’s company. It does not own the warehouse. It owns a derivative of the anxiety of people like Terrence, who are not on salary, who do not have guaranteed hours, who check their phones at 4 a.m. because a robot dispatcher can cut them with no warning, and the robot does not feel bad about it, because robots do not feel bad.

The fund is up seventeen percent. It is the best‑performing product its firm has launched in four years. The portfolio manager, a woman named Jessica who lives in Boston and does not check her phone at 4 a.m., told me the fund is “a pure expression of the structural fragility of the contingent workforce.” She said that into a microphone at a conference where the lunch was salmon.

Terrence does not own the fund. He does not know it exists. If he did, he would understand that the fund does well when he is scared. The fund does very well when he is very scared. It does not do well when he is calm, because nobody has found a way to index calm, because calm does not generate a tradeable spread. Jessica said this was the challenge of the product, and also the beauty of it. She said it with the enthusiasm of a person who has never been cut at 4 a.m.

“A calm person is not a tradeable signal. A calm person does not check their phone at four in the morning. An anxious person checks. That is a price signal. If you are not long the price signal, you are not investing, you are hoping.”

— a night-shift investor who checks the ETF at 4 a.m.
