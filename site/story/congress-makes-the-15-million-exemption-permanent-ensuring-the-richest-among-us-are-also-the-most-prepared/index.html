Heiress With a Staff-Directory Apartment Secures $15 Million Exemption, Explains the Point Is the Money Never Touches Her

Estate planners celebrated the end of the sunset by building trusts for clients who have already outlived the uncertainty they were drafting for.

OZOmar Zayed
Corporate Governance Desk 2026-11-12 · 2 min read

Delia Voss is 31 years old and has not worked a day in her life. She does not say this with shame. She says this, to a reporter, while holding a coffee that costs $14, in an apartment building that has a staff directory posted in the elevator for residents who forget which floor their neighbors live on. Her grandfather built a regional trucking company. Her father sold it. Her lawyers have now ensured that the proceeds of the sale will never touch her and will therefore never be touched by the government. She is, by every legal measure, prepared.

“We met with the estate planner and he explained the dynasty trust as a gift from my grandfather that would keep giving forever,” Delia said. “I asked who the giving goes to. He said me. I asked what I have to do. He said nothing. I asked if that was legal. He said it is the purpose of the law. The law says my grandfather can give me money and the money can sit in a trust and the trust can buy things and the things can be held and the holding can be transferred and the transfer can be exempt. I said exempt from what. He said everything.”

The estate exemption makes the first $15 million of any estate free of federal tax. For Delia’s family, the estate is $214 million. The planner described the remaining $199 million as a “manageable planning exercise.” The exercise involves several more trusts, a family limited partnership with no actual partners, and an insurance policy written by a company Delia has never visited, in a jurisdiction she has never been to, that she will never collect on because nobody in her family has ever needed insurance.

“I asked the lawyer what happens if I have children,” Delia said. “He said the trust will provide for them. I asked what they have to do. He said nothing. I asked if anyone in my family has ever done anything. He said your grandfather drove a truck. I said my grandfather drove a truck. He said that is where the money came from. I asked where the money is going. He said nowhere. He said that is the point. The money is going nowhere. The money is going to me to go nowhere.”

“The $15 million exemption keeps the money where it belongs. My grandfather drove a truck and built something. The government shouldn't take half of it because he died. The trust keeps the family together.”

— a trust beneficiary who believes in generational wealth
