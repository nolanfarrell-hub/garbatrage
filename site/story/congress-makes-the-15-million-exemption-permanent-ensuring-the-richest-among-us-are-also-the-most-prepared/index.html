Congress Makes the $15 Million Exemption Permanent, Ensuring the Richest Among Us Are Also the Most Prepared

Estate planners celebrated the end of the sunset by building trusts for clients who have already outlived the uncertainty they were drafting for.

OZ Omar Zayed
Corporate Governance Desk 2026-11-12 · 2 min read

With the federal estate exemption now permanently fixed, advisors have turned their full attention to the only remaining problem for wealthy families: what to do with the money the government declined to take.

The answer, as ever, is a slightly larger number of documents, including dynasty trusts designed to hold the family's wealth so long that the family itself becomes a minor character in its own estate plan.

Estate attorneys report record demand for irrevocable structures, which they describe as the only way to guarantee that grandchildren will never touch the money, and will instead watch it compound as a form of inheritance their generation can admire from a distance.

Planners were careful to note the work benefits everyone, because an exemption that applies to the very rich is also, technically, applied by the very rich to themselves.

"We are not hiding the money. We are simply making it very clear, generationally, that it never has to leave." — Delia Voss, Third-Generation Heir
