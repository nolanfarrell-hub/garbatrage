Art Built His Office Park In 2004. A Family Office; Bought It In 2026, Explaining The Economy Needs Parking More Than Art

Wealthy families now fund forced sales at a discount, a role regulators described as inevitable and sellers described as generous, eventually.

PS Priya Sharma
Private Capital Reporter 2026-11-15 · 2 min read

Art Delgado built the office park in 2004. He picked the lot, negotiated the loan, and signed the papers himself. Twenty-two years later, the bank that lent him the money told him it was no longer in the commercial real estate lending business, that the building was worth half what he owed, and that if he wanted to keep it he would need to find financing somewhere else. He called every lender he knew. None of them were in the business anymore.

The business was now run by family offices. Wealthy families, some of them so old their money predated the building, had become the largest source of commercial real estate credit in the country. They lent at a discount, bought at a discount, and described their role as capital on standby, which was a polite way of saying they would own the building by spring.

Art sat down with a representative of a family office that controlled more money than the entire county where his building stood. The representative was a young man in a suit that cost more than Art's first car. He asked Art how much he needed. Art told him. The representative said the family would lend him half that and take a 40 percent equity stake, which the representative called a partnership. Art asked what happened if he paid it back. The representative said then Art would own the building again, which was the point of a partnership. Art asked what happened if he could not pay it back. The representative said then the family would own the building, which was also the point of a partnership.

“The young man said this was a generational opportunity to partner with patient capital, and I asked if patient meant they would wait until I died, and he said it meant they would wait until the building was theirs, which was faster, and I said that sounded like a good deal because the building would be in good hands either way.”

— a commercial property owner who values patient capital

At press time, Art was deciding whether to sign the papers or let the bank take the building, a choice the young man described as a win-win, because in both scenarios the family ended up with the building eventually.
