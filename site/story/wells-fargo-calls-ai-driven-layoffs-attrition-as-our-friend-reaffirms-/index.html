Keystone Bank Calls AI-Driven Layoffs 'Attrition As Our Friend,' Reaffirms That Nothing Has Ever Valued The Company As Much As Leaving

The bank plans to shrink its workforce by allowing AI to do what dentures could not, and management says it has only this one friend.

DW Dana Whitfield
Banking Correspondent 2026-11-14 · 2 min read

Darlene Crockett got the email at 9:14 on a Tuesday morning. It said her position was being eliminated through a process called natural attrition, which was the bank's way of saying she would not be replaced when she left, and the bank considered that a friendship. Darlene had worked at Keystone Bank for six years. She had never once been described as a friend by the company. Attrition, apparently, was friendlier than she was.

CEO Charlie Scharf told staff that the bank planned to use attrition as its primary tool for reducing headcount, drawing on a relationship that had quietly been the company's most productive. Attrition did not ask for raises. Attrition did not use the health insurance. Attrition did not sit in on meetings and ask questions about the 401k match. Attrition just sat there, being attrition, saving the bank money without ever once requesting a day off.

Employees across the company received notices that their roles had been reviewed by a model that the bank had recently promoted to analyst on a trial basis. The model found that a significant portion of the work could be performed without the worker. The worker, upon reading this assessment, asked if the model had been informed about mortgages and car payments. The model had not.

“They told me I was being attrited and not laid off, and I told them I appreciated the distinction because attrition sounded like something that happened naturally, like leaves falling, and I was happy to be part of something that happened without anyone having to decide.”

— a former loan processor who supports natural attrition

At press time, Darlene was updating her résumé and wondering whether to list attrition as the reason for leaving, which she decided was honest but sounded like a disease, which, she now understood, it was.
