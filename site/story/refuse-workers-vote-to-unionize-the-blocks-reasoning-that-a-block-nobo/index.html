A Curb In The Third Ward Sent A Possum To Negotiate; The Sanitation Board Has Not Recovered

The garbage trucks stopped coming. The trash piled up. The company tried to break the strike. But the blocks themselves unionized. A curb in the Third Ward now has more bargaining power than the entire sanitation board.

TNTessa Nguyen
Economy & Data Reporter 2026-11-07 · 2 min read

The garbage stopped being picked up. The cans overflowed. The rats had a block party. The company figured they would just scab it, bring in new drivers, run the routes themselves. They did not count on the blocks unionizing.

The blocks are not people. They are locations. They are curbs. They are the spots where the cans sit. And they voted, through a representative who showed up to the meeting, to refuse pickup until the company recognizes the dignity of the curb. The company sent negotiators. The curb sent a possum that looked at them for forty-five minutes.

Shirley Morrison lives on one of these blocks. She put her trash out three weeks ago. It is still there. She is not mad. She says the trash has become a symbol. She does not know what the symbol means, but she has started decorating the bags. The company offered double the usual rate. The block did not respond. The block has no phone.

“The curb has been holding trash for thirty years and nobody asked it what it wanted. Now it is asking. Double the rate is not respect. Double the rate is panic. The curb is not panicking because the curb has all the time in the world.”

— a resident whose trash has been out for twenty-two days

The standoff continues. The trash stays. The company keeps sending offers. The block keeps not accepting them. Shirley Morrison's garbage has become the longest-tenured resident on the street. She is thinking about charging it rent.
