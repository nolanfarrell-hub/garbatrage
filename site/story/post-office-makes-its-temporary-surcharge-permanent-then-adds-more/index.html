Post Office Makes Its Temporary Surcharge Permanent, Then Adds More

The agency has clarified that 'temporary' refers to the period during which the charge is being collected, not the charge itself.

EMElena Marsh
Head of Research 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

The National Mail Service announced Tuesday that its temporary 8% fuel surcharge, implemented in April of this year, has been converted to a permanent fixture of the domestic shipping rate structure, with an additional 3% increment added for what officials describe as 'momentum.' The combined 11% increase — which brings total domestic shipping costs up 5.9% — will take effect January 17, 2027, and applies to every letter, package, and catalog that moves through the system. A Mail Rate Review Board spokesperson confirmed that the word 'temporary' in the original filing referred to the period during which the charge was being collected, not to the duration of the surcharge itself, and that the distinction was 'clearly stated in footnote four of the original document, which ran to 847 pages.'

“The surcharge is 22 cents and I spend more than that on the envelope. If they need to make it permanent to keep the mail running I'd rather pay 22 cents than lose Saturday delivery.”

— a stamp buyer at the counter, 58, who reads the footnotes

The eight-month gap between the temporary implementation and the permanent codification is roughly the same duration it currently takes a first-class letter to travel from Manhattan to Brooklyn, according to internal NMS transit data. Critics of the rate increase — a coalition of three direct-mail marketing executives and a stamp collector from Delaware — have pointed out that the surcharge was originally justified by fuel prices that have since moderated. The Mail Service has responded by noting that 'momentum' is a separate consideration from fuel costs and that the new 3% increment is not a surcharge but a 'rate-structure alignment fee,' which is not subject to the same regulatory review because it has a different name. At press time, the Mail Service was drafting a proposal to reclassify the entire rate schedule as a series of temporary adjustments, thereby freeing the agency from ever having to justify a permanent price change again.
