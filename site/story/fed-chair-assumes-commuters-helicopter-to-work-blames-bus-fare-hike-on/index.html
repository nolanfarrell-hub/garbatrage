Fed Chair Assumes Commuters Helicopter to Work, Blames Bus Fare Hike on Increased Rotor Maintenance

The Federal Reserve's model of transportation inflation rests on a helicopter-commute assumption, an MIT paper on vertical-lift substitution, and a New York Fed officer whose last bus ride was a 1998 field trip to a train museum.

OZ Omar Zayed
Fed Correspondent 2026-11-16 · 2 min read

Intracity transit fares rose 6.6% in October, the largest single-month increase since 2020 and an annualized rate exceeding 100%, a spike the Federal Reserve chair attributed Thursday to rising rotor-maintenance costs in the nation's commuter-helicopter fleet.

At a press conference following the release of the Consumer Price Index, the chair cited "persistent upward pressure on vertical-lift components" and noted that "the helicopter-to-work ratio is holding steady" — a metric no federal statistical agency tracks. The remarks drew on a recent paper published by an MIT economist, "Vertical Lift as a Substitute for Municipal Fixed-Route Transit," which argues that helicopter commuting provides a more accurate measure of transportation inflation than bus fare data. The paper has been accepted for publication and the economist received a departmental bonus.

A New York Fed officer involved in rate-setting decisions told reporters that his personal experience with municipal transit consisted of a 1998 field trip to the New York Transit Museum. "I remember the turnstiles were smaller then," he said, adding that he found the museum's gift shop "educational." The officer declined to say when he had last boarded a bus.

"We have modeled the transportation basket carefully. The rotor-maintenance component is real, and the data do not lie simply because you took the wrong vehicle to work." — Marcus Eldridge, Federal Reserve Board of Governors

At press time, the MIT economist was reportedly revising his paper to include a chapter on gondola subsidies, after the Fed chair mentioned cable-car inflation as an area of "growing concern."
