Fed and the Data Remain in a Closed-Loop Relationship That Does Not Include a Nail Salon Owner

The committee left policy unchanged and noted, for the forty-second straight meeting, that it remains data dependent, which sources confirm is the point.

PS Priya Sharma
Washington Bureau · Fed & Rates 2026-11-12 · 2 min read

Anita Meeks runs a nail salon in a strip mall between a mattress store and a vape shop. She has been watching interest rates for two years because the banker who holds her equipment loan told her to watch them. She watches them on her phone. The rates do not move. The banker calls and says they are data dependent. Anita asks what data. The banker says the data the Fed watches. Anita asks what the Fed watches. The banker says the data. Anita now believes that the Fed and the data are in a relationship that does not include her.

“I asked my banker when the rate will go down,” Anita said. “He said it depends on the data. I asked what data. He said employment, inflation, consumer spending. I asked if my business counts. He said small businesses are in the data. I asked which part. He said the part where people get jobs. I said my customers get jobs. He said that is the data. I said is the data telling the Fed to keep rates high. He said the data is complicated. I said is the data complicated because I am in it. He hung up.”

At the press conference, the Chair said the committee needs to see more progress on inflation. Anita’s rent has gone up 8 percent. Her gel supply costs 12 percent more than last year. Her customers are coming less often. She is the data. The data is telling the Fed something. The Fed is watching the data. The data is watching the Fed. Nobody is watching Anita.

“I called the Federal Reserve,” Anita said. “I asked to speak to someone about the data. They said the Fed does not take calls from the public about the data. I said I am the data. They said the data is submitted by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. I said I am not the Bureau of Labor Statistics. They said then you are not the data. I asked what I am. They said you are a member of the public affected by the data. I asked if that counts. They said it counts in the data. I hung up and looked at my phone. The rate was still 3.875 percent. The nails were still wet.”

“I trust the Fed. They said they need more data before they move. That's responsible. My rate is 3.875 percent and I'm fine with it. I would rather they wait than make a mistake with the whole economy.”

— a small business owner who trusts the data
