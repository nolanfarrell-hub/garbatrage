Family Office Acquires Office Tower Unannounced; Office Manager Now Reports to a Coat Worth More Than Her Car

Forced sales, discounted towers, re-rated everything. The private rich call it a buyer's market; the public funds call it Monday.

TN Tessa Nguyen
Capital Markets Correspondent 2026-11-13 · 2 min read

Janelle Cruz is the office manager for a mid-sized law firm on the seventeenth floor of 200 Madison. She handles the mail, orders the printer toner, and as of two weeks ago, she works for a family office she has never heard of, whose representative showed up unannounced and took measurements of the lobby with a laser.

The representative's name is Delia Voss. She is the third-generation principal of the Voss Family Office. She is thirty-one. She wore a coat that cost more than Janelle's car. She told Janelle that her family had "acquired the building at a highly attractive basis," which Janelle later learned from Google means the building was losing money and the bank wanted out.

The building is losing money because half the floors are empty. The floors are empty because the rent is too high. The rent is too high because the previous owner, a pension fund, needed the building to be worth a certain amount on paper so the pension fund could tell its retirees that everything was fine. The pension fund stopped telling the retirees that. The retirees stopped believing it. Now Delia Voss owns the building, and Janelle's firm is month-to-month, which means the rent can go up at any time, and Delia Voss does not seem like the type who is going to lower it.

Janelle asked Delia if the new ownership meant the broken elevator on the freight side would finally get fixed. Delia said the elevator was a capital expense and the capital was currently allocated to something else. Janelle asked what. Delia said the capital was allocated to acquiring buildings with broken elevators at a discount. Janelle took the stairs.

“Buying buildings nobody wants is how you find value. The mold in the sub-basement is a basis adjustment. That is accounting. The building will be worth more once the market realizes what we paid for it.”

— a building manager who understands distressed assets
