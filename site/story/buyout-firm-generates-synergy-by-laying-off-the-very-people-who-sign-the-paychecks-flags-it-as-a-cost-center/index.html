Private Equity Fires the Person Who Signs the Paychecks, Determines the Hole Is Now a Synergy Hole

A portfolio company announced headcount reductions at its own facilities after the sponsor identified overlap between the employees and the work.

OZOmar Zayed
Corporate Governance 2026-11-12 · 2 min read

Simone Doherty used to work in payroll at a regional grocery distributor owned by a private equity firm called Summit Ridge Capital. She signed the checks. She printed them. She stuffed them in envelopes. She watched the people who cashed them. Last month, Summit Ridge identified an overlap between the employees who signed the paychecks and the employees who received the paychecks, and determined that the overlap was costing the company money. Simone was offered a severance of two weeks and a reminder that her login would expire at 5 PM.

“I asked the operating partner who would sign the checks now,” Simone said. “He said the checks would be signed automatically. I asked who would verify the hours. He said the system would verify the hours. I asked who would fix the system when the system paid someone twice. He said that was a future-synergy opportunity. I said that sounds like a problem. He said problems are opportunities. I asked if the opportunity included my job. He said the opportunity had already been optimized.”

Simone’s position was one of 47 eliminated in the wave. The company saved $1.9 million in salary and benefits. The firm booked the savings as operational improvement. The people who used to cash the checks Simone signed are now paid through a portal that does not have a phone number. The portal is very efficient. The portal does not make mistakes. The portal does not know Mrs. Eldridge’s banana schedule. The portal is the future of payroll.

“I went back to get my things and the woman at the front desk asked me to sign for my last check. I signed it. I handed it back. I said now what. She said now the synergy is complete. I asked if I could use the bathroom. She said the bathroom was a shared resource and costs were being allocated. I said I used that bathroom for 11 years. She said the bathroom is a cost center now. I said what was it before. She said a facility. I said the facility is a bathroom. She said the classification has changed.”

“The bathroom was a shared resource and we were using it for free for eleven years. Of course it's a cost center now. Everything has a cost. That's just accounting catching up to reality.”

— a former employee who understands resource allocation
