Bank Airs Ad of Couple Celebrating They Can Afford the Minimum

Thirteen-point-one percent of credit-card balances are now ninety days late.

CR Claire Reyes
Banking & Credit Reporter 2026-11-17 · 2 min read

Doreen's balance is nineteen months old and she still pays only the minimum. She is, by the industry's measure, thriving. Thirteen-point-one percent of all card balances are now ninety days past due, the highest share since 2011, and to the ordinary eye this looks like distress. To the industry it looks like a customer who finally understands the product; Meridian Consumer Financial calls it 'revolving engagement.'

Doreen calls the minimum her hook, the way she keeps the balance alive without once having to say out loud that she cannot pay it off. "The bank wants the intimacy," she said, repeating a phrase from the campaign mailer she keeps on the fridge. "They used to have to entice people into the balance. Now we run toward it."

Doreen pays $187 a month in interest and fees, up from $112 two years ago, which is the industry gently milking a teat it has been told is the udder. Revolving debt is $1.4 trillion; 43 percent of accounts pay only the minimum. The scale tells the industry the ideal customer carries a balance forever, pays the minimum, and occasionally misses a payment so the bank can show its forbearance programs. That is a relationship, the mailer says, by which it means a hand that never quite lets go of the wrist.

His new campaign shows a couple celebrating because they could afford the minimum. Later this month, the second spot: an older couple dancing because their interest was capitalized. Tagline: 'You're On Your Way.' Doreen is certainly not off it.

“I pay the minimum every month and my credit score is 740. The bank keeps raising my limit. If they thought I couldn't handle it they wouldn't keep sending me offers for more credit.”

— a cardholder, 37, who has carried a balance for six years
