Berkshire Bought Back $9.6 Billion Of Its Own Stock In A Quarter Its Own Operating Profits Fell, Citing Confidence In A Product It Controls The Audience For

The conglomerate more than doubled repurchases while insurance underwriting tumbled 43%, describing the buyback as an expression of long-term conviction in a security whose only shareholders are, increasingly, the same company.

MR Marcus Reid
Markets Anchor · Industrials 2026-11-09 · 7 min read

Berkshire Hathaway repurchased $9.6 billion of its own stock in the third quarter — more than double the prior quarter — even as operating profits fell 5.8% and insurance underwriting returned to the earth. Management framed the buyback as an act of confidence, a description shareholders accepted on the grounds that the buyer and the seller were, functionally, family.

The arithmetic is clean enough: when a company buys its own shares and no one else is buying, the remaining holders get a larger slice of a slightly smaller pie, which the market generally files under 'returning capital to shareholders,' a phrase that quietly includes the shareholder doing the returning.

Operating earnings declined as insurance investment income slid 14%, and total earnings dropped 28% when the stock portfolio stopped doing the heavy lifting. None of it slowed the repurchase machine, because a buyback does not require profits to justify itself — only the belief that the shares, having fallen, represent better value than whatever the money would do sitting next to them.

Analysts note the deep logic of the cycle: the company's own stock is its most dependable acquisition target, unencumbered by due diligence, antitrust review, or the inconvenient existence of a seller with leverage. 'You never overpay for your own company,' one wrote, 'because the board has already approved the price and the counterparty has never objected.'

The $14.4 billion year-to-date total is on pace to set a record that will be announced, shareholders suspect, to a room composed largely of the company.
